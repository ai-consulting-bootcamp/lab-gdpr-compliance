## Transition note (from the EU AI Act lab)

I'm carrying forward **Case 2** from my partner's AI Act lab: a logistics company that wants an AI tool to score job applicants. In the AI Act lab this case fell into the **high-risk tier** (Annex III, point 4(a): AI used to filter applications and evaluate candidates). Of the four cases it's the most interesting one for GDPR. It combines a large volume of applicant data, a score that effectively decides who gets rejected (Art. 22), training data built from current employees' performance, cross-border hiring in Germany and Poland, and a US vendor, so every part of the audit has something real to test. (Case 1, emotion recognition at work, is prohibited outright under AI Act Art. 5(1)(f), so its GDPR audit ends quickly at "stop".)

## Fact pattern

**Client:** *Kranich Logistik GmbH* (name fictional), a logistics company with warehouses in Germany and Poland (Polish subsidiary *Kranich Logistyka sp. z o.o.*). It hires about 400 people a year, from forklift drivers to shift managers, with two recruiters. Roles get up to 300 applications each, so **tens of thousands of applications a year.**

**Personal data:** CVs and cover letters, meaning name, contact details, work history, education, licences (e.g. forklift certificate), skills, and often a photo, date of birth, nationality, gaps in employment or disability mentions. Plus **"top performer" profiles**: performance and career data on current and former employees from the last five years. The tool's output is a **score per applicant**.

**Data subjects:** applicants (mostly in Germany and Poland, some applying from other EU and non-EU countries) and current and former employees whose data forms the top-performer profiles.

**Vendors:** a **US-based** AI screening vendor (*fictional for this lab*), connected by API to the client's applicant portal.

**What the AI does:** it reads CVs and cover letters, compares them with the job description and the top-performer profiles, and scores each applicant. Recruiters open only the top 20. **Everyone else gets an automatic rejection email after a week.** Recruiters *can* pull candidates up manually but rarely have time to.

**Automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects?** **Yes.** Rejection from a job is a similarly significant effect, and for everyone below the top 20 no human is meaningfully involved.

## Section B — Risk and rights

**Special-category data (Art. 9)?**
Yes, likely. Photos, health mentions and religion appear in CVs, and protected traits are **inferable** from proxies. Art. 22(4) bars solely automated decisions based on Art. 9 data in almost all cases. Mitigation: strip photos, date of birth, nationality and free-text health mentions before scoring; run bias tests on outcomes by gender, age and nationality (also needed under the German anti-discrimination law AGG and the Polish Labour Code art. 18³ᵃ).

**Automated decision-making (Art. 22)?**
**Yes, as designed.** The auto-rejection of everyone outside the top 20 is a decision based solely on automated processing: recruiters "rarely" look below the cut-off, and a human who doesn't actually review is not meaningful involvement. Under CJEU *SCHUFA* (C-634/21, 2023), a score that decisively drives a decision counts as the decision. **Safeguard:** either (a) a recruiter reviews every rejection with real authority and time, or (b) rely on Art. 22(2)(a) with Art. 22(3) safeguards: a right to human review, a way to contest, and an explanation of the main factors in the rejection email.

**Is a DPIA required?**
**Yes, clearly.** 7 of the EDPB's 9 criteria apply:
1. **Evaluation/scoring:** applicant scoring ✅
2. **Automated decision with legal or similar effect:** auto-rejection ✅
3. Systematic monitoring: ❌
4. **Sensitive data:** photos, inferred traits, employee absence data ✅
5. **Large scale:** tens of thousands of applicants a year ✅
6. **Matching/combining datasets:** applicant data × employee performance data ✅
7. **Vulnerable data subjects:** applicants and employees face a power imbalance with the employer ✅
8. **Innovative technology:** AI/LLM-based CV parsing and scoring ✅
9. **Prevents access to a contract:** blocks access to employment ✅ (counted under criterion 2)

**Likely data-subject friction points**
- **Right of access (Art. 15) + explanation:** rejected candidates asking "why was I rejected?" and requesting their score. Art. 15(1)(h) requires meaningful information about the logic involved.
- **Right to contest / human review (Art. 22(3)):** expect challenges, especially from strong candidates rejected automatically.
- **Right to object (Art. 21):** employees objecting to their performance data being used in top-performer profiles.
- **Erasure (Art. 17):** candidates asking for deletion before the 6-month retention period ends. The client can refuse only where it still needs the data to defend legal claims.

## Section C — Law stacking

- **AI Act:** **High-risk** (Annex III 4(a)). The AI Act adds obligations GDPR doesn't cover. As **deployer**, the client must: use the system per the vendor's instructions; assign **trained human oversight** with the power to override; monitor it and **keep logs**; **inform workers' representatives** before deployment (Art. 26(7)); **inform applicants** that a high-risk AI system is used on them (Art. 26(11)); and, on request, give an explanation of the decision (Art. 86). The vendor, as provider, needs conformity assessment, CE marking and EU database registration. **Timing:** the AI Omnibus (Reg. (EU) 2026/1744) moved Annex III high-risk obligations to **2 December 2027**. A Q1 2027 launch is therefore earlier than the legal deadline, but the system has to comply from that date, so build it in now. AI literacy (Art. 4) already applies.
- **ePrivacy:** only marginal. The applicant portal may set cookies; non-essential ones need consent (DE: TDDDG §25). It doesn't affect the core scoring analysis.
- **Data Act:** **N/A.** There are no connected products and no cloud-switching data in this use case.
- *(Also noted)* **German works constitution law (BetrVG):** the works council has co-determination rights over technical systems that can monitor performance (§87(1) no. 6) and over **selection guidelines** (§95). An AI scoring model is a selection guideline, so the **works council must agree before rollout**. Q1 is tight. **Anti-discrimination:** AGG (DE) and Labour Code art. 18³ᵃ (PL) put the burden on the employer once a rejected candidate shows indications of discrimination.

# PART 2 — CLIENT RECOMMENDATION MEMO

**To:** HR Director, Legal and IT, Kranich Logistik GmbH
**From:** Pieter Albrecht, Privacy & AI Advisory
**Re:** GDPR review of the AI CV-screening tool
**Date:** 17 November 2026

**Bottom line: Go with conditions, but not in the current design.** Automatically rejecting everyone outside the top 20 is a decision made solely by a machine, which GDPR prohibits without strict safeguards. A redesigned process can work.

**What we found**
Using AI to sort a high volume of applications is legitimate, and an AI score that recruiters use to set their reading order is defensible. The problem is the cut-off: a person who is never read by a human and gets a form rejection has been judged by the algorithm alone. Two further risks: the "top performer" profiles may teach the tool to repeat past hiring bias, and applicant data goes to a US vendor.

**Top three actions, in this order**
1. **Now:** brief your works council and start negotiating a works agreement. The scoring criteria count as selection guidelines, so nothing goes live without their agreement. That makes the Q1 date realistic only if you start this month.
2. **Before any data flows:** sign a data processing agreement with the vendor that bars it from training its own models on your data, check its EU-US Data Privacy Framework certification and complete a DPIA. Include a bias test of the top-performer profiles and remove photos, date of birth and nationality before scoring.
3. **Before launch:** replace the automatic rejection with a recruiter sign-off, supported by a short reason from the tool. Tell applicants that AI is used and offer a human review on request. Keep the 6-month retention and deletion rule.

**Risks that remain even if you do all this**
- **Rubber-stamping.** With two recruiters and 300 applications per role, sign-off can turn into clicking "confirm". Track override rates; if they stay at zero, regulators will treat the review as fake.
- **Hidden bias.** Removing photos and dates of birth doesn't remove proxies like career gaps or place of birth. Testing reduces the risk of discrimination claims but can't rule it out.
- **US transfer.** The EU-US framework is under appeal at the EU Court of Justice. Keep standard contractual clauses ready, or ask the vendor for EU-only processing.

The AI Act's high-risk obligations for hiring tools apply from December 2027. Building them in now avoids having to redesign the process later.

*Pieter Albrecht*
