# GDPR Audit Pack – Smartwatch Health Data Analytics

## Transition Note

This assessment continues Case 2 from our previous EU AI Act lab. The proposed application was classified as high-risk because AI-generated health profiles would be used for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance (Annex III, point 5(c)). The scenario is particularly relevant for GDPR analysis due to the processing of sensitive health information, individual profiling, and potential sharing of personal data with third parties.

## Phase 1 – Fact Pattern

A medium-sized European smartwatch manufacturer wants to develop an AI-powered health analytics service for approximately 50,000 customers located in the EU/EEA.

The system would collect and analyze health-related information, including heart rate, sleep patterns, physical activity, stress indicators, and other measurements recorded by the smartwatches.

Using machine learning, the company plans to generate individual health profiles and predictive risk scores that could be shared with life and health insurance providers for insurance pricing and eligibility assessments.

Users would be offered separate opt-in choices for AI-powered health analysis and sharing their results with insurance partners. Insurance employees would review AI-generated assessments before making final underwriting decisions.

The company plans to use European cloud infrastructure and a US-based AI analytics provider, potentially involving international data transfers.

The proposed service would create an additional revenue stream through insurance partnerships and potentially through a premium subscription offering personal health insights.

### Section B – Risk and Rights

#### 1. Are any special-category data present or inferable from the outputs (Article 9)?

Yes. Heart rate, sleep patterns, stress indicators, and AI-generated health risk profiles qualify as health-related personal data under Article 9 GDPR. The derived risk scores may also reveal sensitive information about an individual's physical or mental health.

Processing these data requires a valid Article 6 lawful basis and an additional Article 9 exception, potentially explicit consent under Article 9(2)(a).

#### 2. Is there automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects (Article 22)?

Potentially yes. If insurance companies use AI-generated risk scores to determine insurance eligibility or premiums without meaningful human involvement, Article 22 restrictions may apply.

A human reviewer must have sufficient information, authority, and time to challenge or override AI recommendations. Merely approving an automated recommendation without genuine evaluation is insufficient.

Solely automated decisions involving health data would require particularly careful legal assessment under Article 22(4).

#### 3. Is a DPIA required? Use the EDPB's nine criteria.

**Assessment: Yes – a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is required before processing begins.**

The proposed application meets several high-risk indicators established by the EDPB-endorsed DPIA guidelines.

| EDPB criterion | Applies? | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Evaluation or scoring | Yes | AI generates individual health risk scores. |
| 2. Automated decisions with significant effects | Potentially | Insurance eligibility and pricing may be affected. |
| 3. Systematic monitoring | Yes | Smartwatches continuously collect personal health and activity information. |
| 4. Sensitive or highly personal data | Yes | Health data fall under Article 9 GDPR. |
| 5. Large-scale processing | Likely | Approximately 50,000 users are involved. |
| 6. Matching or combining datasets | Yes | Multiple health indicators and user information are combined. |
| 7. Vulnerable data subjects | Potentially | Some users may be particularly vulnerable due to health conditions or dependency on insurance. |
| 8. Innovative technology | Yes | Machine learning generates predictive health assessments. |
| 9. Prevention of access to a service or contract | Potentially | Risk scores could negatively affect insurance access or pricing. |

Multiple criteria are met, making a DPIA necessary under Article 35 GDPR.

The DPIA must assess necessity, proportionality, risks to individuals, and appropriate safeguards. If high residual risks cannot be sufficiently mitigated, prior consultation with the supervisory authority may be required under Article 36.

#### 4. What data subject friction points are most likely?

The most relevant GDPR rights include:

- **Right of access (Article 15):** Users may request information about their health data, AI-generated profiles, and recipients.
- **Right to rectification (Article 16):** Users may challenge inaccurate personal information and potentially incorrect inferred profiles.
- **Right to erasure (Article 17):** Users may request deletion where the legal conditions apply.
- **Right to withdraw consent (Article 7):** Users must be able to withdraw consent without unnecessary obstacles.
- **Right to object (Article 21):** Relevant where processing is based on legitimate interests or another applicable ground covered by Article 21.
- **Automated decision-making safeguards (Article 22):** Where applicable, users must have appropriate protections against solely automated significant decisions.

The greatest challenge will be ensuring that users understand how their data influence insurance assessments and can exercise their rights effectively.

#### 5. What is the controller / processor split?

| Entity | GDPR role | Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Smartwatch users | Data subjects | Individuals whose personal information is processed. |
| Smartwatch manufacturer | Controller | Determines purposes and essential means of health analytics and commercial data sharing. |
| AI analytics provider | Processor, if acting solely on instructions | Processes health data for the manufacturer. |
| European cloud hosting provider | Processor or sub-processor | Hosts and stores data according to the contractual structure. |
| Insurance companies | Generally independent controllers | Determine how health risk profiles are used for insurance decisions. |

If the manufacturer and insurers jointly determine the purposes and essential means of processing, joint controllership under Article 26 must be assessed.

If an AI vendor independently reuses health data for its own purposes, its role may change and must be reviewed.

#### 6. Is a Data Processing Agreement (DPA) needed?

Yes. Article 28 GDPR requires appropriate data processing agreements with vendors acting as processors.

Required arrangements may include:

- A DPA between the smartwatch manufacturer and the AI analytics provider.
- A DPA with the cloud hosting provider, depending on the processing structure.
- Authorization and contractual safeguards for any sub-processors.

Insurance companies acting as independent controllers generally require an appropriate controller-to-controller data-sharing arrangement rather than a processor DPA.

Contracts must address processing instructions, confidentiality, security, sub-processing, deletion, audit rights, and assistance with GDPR obligations.

### Section C – Law Stacking

#### 1. EU AI Act Cross-Check

**Classification: High Risk – Annex III, Point 5(c).**

AI-generated health risk assessments intended for pricing or risk evaluation in life and health insurance fall within the EU AI Act's high-risk category.

The AI Act adds requirements beyond GDPR, including AI risk management, technical documentation, data governance, logging, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, and conformity assessment.

The smartwatch manufacturer may be the AI system provider, while the insurance company acts as the deployer. Depending on the actual contractual and technical arrangements, these roles must be verified.

A Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA) is required for qualifying deployers of AI systems covered by Annex III, point 5(c).

Under the amended AI Act timeline, the main Annex III high-risk requirements become applicable on December 2, 2027. GDPR requirements already apply.

#### 2. ePrivacy Check

**Relevant – further technical assessment required.**

The smartwatch collects information through sensors and connected applications.

Access to information stored on users' devices may trigger Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive and relevant national implementation rules. Consent may be required unless a specific exemption applies.

The company must examine how its smartwatch application accesses and transfers device-level information.

No website tracking cookies are specified in the current scenario, but any additional analytics or tracking technologies must also be assessed.

Compliance with ePrivacy requirements does not remove the need for a separate GDPR lawful basis where personal data are processed.

#### 3. EU Data Act Check

**Relevant – connected product data.**

Smartwatches qualify as connected products within the scope of the EU Data Act.

The Data Act grants users certain rights to access and share readily available data generated through the use of connected devices.

Raw and pre-processed smartwatch data may fall within these provisions, while AI-inferred or derived health risk scores generally fall outside the mandatory data-sharing scope.

The manufacturer must assess applicable user access and sharing rights. The Data Act does not automatically authorize the commercial sale of personal health data to insurance companies.

GDPR protections continue to apply.

## Phase 3 – Client Recommendation Memo

**To:** Smartwatch Manufacturer – Management, Legal and Technology Teams

**Subject:** GDPR Assessment of AI-Powered Health Data Analytics

**Recommendation: STOP the proposed insurance data-sharing launch until the identified legal and privacy requirements have been addressed.**

### Executive Assessment

Our assessment identified significant GDPR risks associated with the proposed AI-powered health analytics platform.

The system would process sensitive health information from approximately 50,000 EU users, generate predictive health risk scores, and potentially share these assessments with insurance providers.

Although the technology offers commercial opportunities, its intended use introduces substantial risks concerning data protection, individual profiling, automated decisions, and international data transfers.

We recommend proceeding with a controlled development and compliance preparation phase, but not launching the insurance data-sharing functionality until the required safeguards are established.

### Top Three Required Actions

**1. Establish lawful processing and consent management.**

Separate the purposes of personal health analytics and insurance-related data sharing. Determine and document valid lawful grounds under Articles 6 and 9 GDPR.

Where consent is relied upon, implement explicit, informed, freely given, and withdrawable consent mechanisms. Users must be able to use the personal analytics service without being forced to share their information with insurance partners.

**2. Conduct a DPIA and implement technical safeguards.**

Complete a Data Protection Impact Assessment before processing begins.

Implement encryption, access controls, data minimization, defined retention periods, and meaningful human oversight for insurance-related decisions.

Ensure users can access, correct, and request deletion of their information where applicable.

**3. Verify vendor contracts and international data transfers.**

Establish appropriate Data Processing Agreements with AI and cloud service providers.

Review all international data flows, including possible transfers to the US-based analytics provider.

Verify whether an adequacy mechanism or Standard Contractual Clauses and additional safeguards are required.

### Residual Risks

Even after implementing these measures, several risks remain:

- **Inaccurate AI predictions:** Incorrect risk scores could unfairly influence insurance decisions.
- **Privacy and security risks:** Sensitive health profiles remain attractive targets for unauthorized access and misuse.
- **Questionable consent validity:** Commercial incentives or insurance-related pressure could undermine the voluntary nature of consent.

### Final Recommendation

The company should initially prioritize an optional personal health analytics service without insurance data sharing.

Expansion into insurance partnerships should occur only after successful DPIA completion, legal validation of data processing, effective safeguards, and verification of applicable EU AI Act requirements.

**Final Status: STOP AS PROPOSED – CONDITIONAL REASSESSMENT AFTER REDESIGN AND COMPLIANCE REVIEW.**

### Overall Assessment

The recommendation memo provides a clear and practical initial compliance assessment. Its main strength is the identification of realistic organizational and technical safeguards before processing actual banking data.

The recommendation to remove personal identifiers before sending information to an external LLM and restrict reporting to process-level insights is particularly valuable.

However, several questions require clarification:

1. **Lawful basis:** Which Article 6 GDPR lawful basis applies to the processing of employee and customer information? Does the potential inclusion of health information or other special-category data require an Article 9 exception?
2. **International data transfers:** What transfer mechanism applies if personal data are accessible from outside the EU? Vendor due diligence should explicitly verify data locations, subprocessors, and relevant transfer safeguards.
3. **Responsibility and transparency:** The provider/deployer classification should be verified against the actual development and deployment arrangements. The proposed obligation to label AI-generated text also needs a more specific assessment under Article 50.

### Client Response

We accept the recommendation to proceed with development using synthetic data while postponing the processing of real personal information until the necessary safeguards have been established.

Before approving deployment, we request clarification of the GDPR lawful basis for employee and customer data, the international data transfer mechanisms, and the contractual responsibilities of the external AI provider.

We also expect the DPIA and relevant stakeholder reviews to be completed before the system is introduced into production.

## Phase 5 – Debrief

### Main Findings

The assessment confirms that the most significant GDPR challenges involve special-category health data, individual profiling, consent validity, and the potential consequences of insurance-related decisions.

The strongest recommendation is to separate personal health analytics from commercial insurance data sharing. This would reduce the immediate compliance complexity and allow the company to evaluate a potentially less intrusive business model.

### Open Questions for Legal Review

- Can consent for health data monetization be considered genuinely voluntary in the proposed business model?
- What exact lawful bases and Article 9 exceptions apply to each party and processing purpose?
- Would the insurer's decision-making process trigger Article 22 restrictions?
- Which insurance-specific national laws impose additional limitations?
- Which international transfer safeguards are necessary for the selected AI vendor?

### Final Learning

The EU AI Act determines the regulatory requirements associated with the AI system and its intended use, while GDPR determines whether the underlying personal data processing is lawful.

Meeting the AI Act's requirements does not automatically establish GDPR compliance.

**Key takeaway:** A technically feasible and potentially AI Act-compliant solution may still be unsuitable for launch if its personal data processing lacks a valid legal basis or creates unacceptable privacy risks.
